01 Red-flag symptoms Assessed first. If any is present, the patient returns to the ED regardless of the aneurysm characteristics.

This is already a return to the ED. A red-flag symptom of this kind takes precedence over the aneurysm characteristics. The remaining questions may be completed for the record, but the disposition will not change.

Was a lumbar puncture performed for this headache at the first ED visit?

Thunderclap headache without a prior LP means bleeding has not been excluded, which is a return to the ED. A completed LP allows the assessment to continue to the lesion.

02 Location of the finding Cavernous-segment lesions lie outside the dura, so a rupture would not cause a subarachnoid bleed. Posterior-circulation lesions are escalated one tier.

Are cavernous-sinus symptoms present? 3rd, 4th or 6th nerve palsy, retro-orbital pain

10 mm or larger is an automatic return to the ED. A band is sufficient for the recommendation; an exact measurement is recorded on the printout if one is entered.

Does it meet all classic-infundibulum criteria, at a typical location?

Under 3 mm · smooth funnel · branch vessel from the apex · typical branch point · no bleb or lobulation. All criteria must be met to distinguish a benign infundibulum from a small aneurysm.

Imaging characteristics do not affect the disposition for this location. The cavernous segment lies outside the dura and carries no risk of a subarachnoid bleed. The disposition is determined entirely by the cavernous-sinus symptoms question above.

04 Vascular risk factors Two or more classifies the patient as higher-risk and can raise urgency by one tier.

05 Patient age Adjusts the recommendation. A younger patient keeps a lower threshold for referral; a patient of advanced age with a small, benign-appearing lesion may require only reassurance. Never overrides a return to the ED.
